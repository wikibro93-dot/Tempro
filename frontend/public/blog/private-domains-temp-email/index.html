The alternative route: forwarding to your real inbox

Premium is not the only way to use a private domain. The older route is forwarding:

1. Buy a domain from a registrar such as Namecheap, GoDaddy, or Google Domains. 2. Set up email forwarding with a service such as ImprovMX or ForwardEmail, forwarding mail to your real inbox. 3. Add a catch-all feature so any random address under your domain works. Example: anything@mydomain.com → forwards to your real inbox. 4. Secure with filters — add filters or rules in Gmail or Outlook to keep spam away.

This route works, and it needs no TempMail Pro subscription. But read the trade-off below before choosing it, because the two routes send your mail to very different places.

Forwarding to a real inbox vs keeping mail in a disposable inbox

This is the decision that matters most, and it is easy to get backwards:

| | Forwarding to your real inbox | Disposable inbox (Premium BYOD) | | --- | --- | --- | | Where mail lives | Your permanent inbox, mixed with your real mail | A separate disposable inbox, isolated from your real mail | | After a signup leaks or turns spammy | The spam follows you into your real inbox until you filter or retire the address | Retire that one inbox; your real inbox never saw it | | Long-term access | Mail stays searchable in your real inbox for months or years | Mail ends with the inbox — save anything you need before expiry | | Separation | Depends on filters you maintain | Built in — one inbox per signup by design | | Best for | Addresses you want to keep receiving on long-term, such as a professional contact address | Signups you want to trace, test, or abandon without a trace in your main inbox |

In short: forwarding is for mail you want to keep; a disposable inbox is for mail you want to contain. If your goal is privacy — keeping signup mail out of your real inbox entirely — forwarding defeats the purpose, because every message still lands in the inbox you were trying to protect. If your goal is permanence — an address that still receives mail a year from now — a disposable inbox is the wrong tool, because it is designed to end. Pick the route that matches the goal, not the one with the easier setup page.

A custom name is not a private domain — know which one you need

TempMail Pro lets you pick a custom name for a temporary inbox, which is useful for tracing signups. That is not the same as owning a private domain, and confusing the two leads to bad decisions. A custom name on a shared temp-mail domain still lives on someone else's domain, expires with the inbox, and cannot receive mail after expiry. A private domain is yours: you control its addresses, its forwarding, and how long they live. Note also that a custom name gets a random 4-character suffix appended, so the exact name you typed is not guaranteed.

| Need | Custom temp name | Your own private domain | | --- | --- | --- | | Try a service for a day | Yes, ideal | Overkill | | Trace which signup sent what | Yes, for the inbox lifetime | Yes, permanently | | Receive mail months later | No — inbox expires | Yes, as long as you keep the domain | | Look professional or branded | No | Yes | | Effort and cost | None | Domain cost plus setup time |

The catch-all trap

A catch-all address — where anything@yourdomain.com reaches you — is convenient and risky in equal measure. It means every guessable address works, including addresses spammers guess, addresses old signups leak, and addresses you never created. If you run a catch-all, pair it with filters from day one: route unknown senders to a separate folder, review it weekly, and retire addresses that start attracting spam instead of letting them accumulate. Without filters, a catch-all quietly becomes the same messy inbox a private domain was supposed to replace. On the Premium route, prefer deliberate per-signup inboxes over a catch-all — you have 10 inbox slots, so spend them on signups you can name and trace.

When a private domain is overkill

For most people, most of the time, it is. If your actual need is trying trials, unlocking coupons, or testing signups, a temporary inbox with a custom name does the job with zero setup and zero ongoing cost. A private domain earns its keep only when you do this work repeatedly at volume — running many ongoing aliases, managing a business identity, or needing addresses that survive for months. Buying a domain for a single weekend trial is like renting an office to write one email.

A sensible setup checklist

1. Decide which route fits your goal first — Premium disposable inboxes for containment, forwarding for permanence — before buying anything. 2. Buy a domain you can keep renewing — a lapsed domain can be re-registered by someone else, with your old addresses still pointing at it in old signups. 3. If you choose Premium, connect the domain in the app and create inboxes deliberately, one per signup, keeping an eye on the 10-inbox and 50-email monthly limits. 4. If you choose forwarding, set it up to an inbox you actually check, not to an address you ignore. 5. Use a catch-all only with filters, or skip it and create addresses deliberately per signup. 6. Keep a list of which address went to which service, so a breach or spam wave can be traced and that one address retired. 7. Never use a private-domain address for banking or recovery-critical accounts unless that domain's email is itself secured and monitored long-term.
