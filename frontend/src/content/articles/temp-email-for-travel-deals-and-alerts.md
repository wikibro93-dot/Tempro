## A fare sale should not follow you home

You search for flights one evening, sign up for a price alert so you do not miss a drop, and then the trip is booked — or abandoned — while the emails keep coming. Months later you are still getting hotel offers for a city you never visited and "last chance" fares for dates that have already passed. The alert did its job in a single week, but the signup lived on far longer than the search.

A temporary inbox fits this pattern well, because a travel search usually has a clear end date. You need messages for a short window: confirmation of the alert, price-change notices, and perhaps a promo code. After that, you do not need the sender to keep writing to you. TempMail Pro gives you a free temporary address with no sign-in, so you can create an inbox for one trip search, watch it while you decide, and let it expire when the search is over.

## Named alert and deal services: what each one sends you

Travel alerts are not all the same kind of mail. Some send a price change for a route you chose; others send a daily digest of deals you did not ask for. Knowing which is which decides the expiry.

**Google Flights price tracking.** You pick a route and dates, turn on tracking, and Google Flights emails you when the price changes. The mail is specific: one route, one price movement. A temporary inbox fits well, because the alert has a clear end — the day you book or abandon the route. Sign up with a temp address, watch for the drop, and let the inbox expire when the search ends.

**Skyscanner price alerts.** Skyscanner works the same way: set an alert for a route and dates, and get emailed when the price moves. The alert is tied to the search, not to an account you need to keep, so a temporary inbox covers it cleanly.

**Kayak price alerts.** Kayak lets you set a price alert for a flight or a hotel, and emails you when the price changes or when its fare forecast changes. Like the two above, it is a search-scoped alert with a natural end date.

**Hopper.** Hopper's core feature is price prediction — it tells you whether to book now or wait — and it sends notifications when the price is expected to move. Hopper is app-first, so much of this arrives as a push notification rather than email; the email signup is usually for the account and for deal digests. A temp inbox works for the digest sampling, but the app itself needs a real account, so do not expect a disposable address to carry the whole Hopper experience.

**Going (formerly Scott's Cheap Flights).** Going sends a curated list of mistake fares and cheap flights from your home airports, usually by email. This is a newsletter, not a route alert: it keeps coming whether or not you are searching. Sample it with a 7-day or custom 30-day inbox, and if you keep opening it, move it to a permanent address or an alias. The sampling workflow is the same one described for newsletters in [Managing Email Subscriptions: Temporary Email for Newsletters](/blog/temp-email-for-newsletters).

**Secret Flying and Airfarewatchdog.** Both send deal alerts by email — Secret Flying for mistake fares and error fares, Airfarewatchdog for fare drops on routes you follow. Both are deal digests rather than single-route trackers, so the same sampling rule applies: try them on a temporary inbox for a week or two, keep the ones you actually read, let the rest expire.

The split to remember is route alerts versus deal digests. Route alerts end when the trip is decided, so a short expiry fits. Deal digests have no end date, so they need a sampling window and a decision at the end of it.

## What a temporary inbox is good for here

Use it for the exploratory, short-lived parts of travel planning:

- Price-drop alerts for a specific route and date range you are actively comparing
- Deal newsletters you want to sample for one or two weeks before a trip
- Signups that unlock a fare list, a hotel coupon, or a destination guide
- Separate alerts for different trips, so a beach holiday and a work trip do not mix

This is different from a booking itself. If you are actually buying a ticket or reserving a room, the confirmation, itinerary changes, and check-in details should go to an address you will still control on travel day. Keep the temporary inbox for the search and the alerts, and keep the purchase on your permanent address.

## One inbox per trip

Mixing every destination into a single inbox recreates the clutter you were trying to avoid. A cleaner method is one inbox per trip, named for the trip itself. Name the inbox in plain words you will recognise later, without putting personal details in the name. When the trip search ends, delete the inbox or let it expire, and start fresh for the next search rather than reviving an old address for a new destination.

## An expiry-per-trip system

TempMail Pro offers 10 minutes, 1 hour, 24 hours, 7 days, or a custom duration up to 365 days. Pick the shortest duration that still covers the job — and make the decision per trip, not per service:

| Trip stage | Suggested expiry | Why it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One fare check or a single download | 10 minutes to 1 hour | You only need a confirmation or a link |
| A weekend of comparing options | 24 hours | Alerts arrive across a day or two |
| A trip you are watching for a week | 7 days | Price movement takes time to show |
| A trip you are planning over a month | Custom 30 days | Covers the full comparison window |
| A "someday" trip you revisit | Custom, with a review date | Revisit the search rather than leaving it open |
| A deal digest you are sampling | 7 days, then decide | Keep it or let it expire at the end of the sample |

Two examples make the system concrete. A US domestic trip you are booking this week — say, New York to Chicago next month — gets one inbox with a 7-day expiry on Google Flights or Kayak: long enough to catch a price drop, short enough to close when you book. A "someday" trip to Lisbon with no dates gets a custom 30-day inbox on a deal digest like Going: if you are still opening the digest at day 30, it earns a permanent address; if not, it expires and the dream-trip mail stops.

Expired inboxes and their messages are assumed unrecoverable, so save anything you still need before expiry. If a fare alert contains a code you plan to use, copy that code into your own notes while the inbox is still live. Do not leave the only copy of a booking reference inside an inbox that is about to expire.

## A practical workflow for a price alert

1. Decide the route, the dates, and the price you would actually pay, in dollars if that is how the fare is shown, before you sign up for anything.
2. Create a temporary inbox named for the trip and set an expiry that covers your decision window.
3. Sign up for the alert with that address, then confirm the signup from inside the temporary inbox.
4. Check the inbox once or twice a day while you are deciding, rather than leaving alerts unread until the fare has moved.
5. When the price hits your target, book with your permanent address, and save the confirmation outside the temporary inbox.
6. When the search ends, delete the temporary inbox. If the trip is postponed, create a fresh inbox when you restart the search.

Time zones matter more than people expect. A fare alert timed for morning Eastern time may land in the middle of the night in Pacific time, and a "today only" offer may expire sooner than it appears to. Act on the sender's stated deadline rather than assuming you have until the end of your own day. Once a trip moves from "maybe" to "booked," let your permanent inbox take over — confirmations and check-in updates need an address that will still exist on the day you travel.

## How this stays distinct from shopping alerts

Travel alerts and shopping deal alerts look similar — both are price watches — but they differ in one important way: a travel search has a fixed end date (the trip), while a shopping habit does not. That is why this article's system is built around the trip, not the store: one inbox per trip, an expiry matched to the trip's decision window, and a hard switch to a permanent address the moment money changes hands.
