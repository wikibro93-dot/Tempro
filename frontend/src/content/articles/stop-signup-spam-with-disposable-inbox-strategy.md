## Why unsubscribing never quite works

Most signup spam is not a single newsletter you can unsubscribe from. It is a chain: one signup feeds a welcome series, which feeds partner offers, which feeds retargeting, and each list has its own unsubscribe process, its own delays, and its own definition of "unsubscribe." You can spend real time opting out and still receive mail from the same company under a different list. The disposable-inbox strategy does not fight that chain — it stops your personal address from entering it in the first place.

## The three-tier system

Sort every signup into one of three tiers before you hand over an address:

**Tier 1 — Permanent address, for trusted long-term services.** Your bank, your employer, services you have used for years, anything needing password recovery. This tier should be small, and it should only grow deliberately.

**Tier 2 — Alias, for accounts you may keep.** An alias forwards to a mailbox you control but keeps your main address hidden and lets you filter or disable one alias without affecting the rest. Use it for services that are useful but not fully trusted, or for accounts whose future you are unsure about.

**Tier 3 — Temporary inbox, for everything else.** Coupons, trials, one-time downloads, unfamiliar sites, flash sales, newsletter tests. These are the signups that generate most spam, and they are exactly the ones that do not need a permanent address. A temporary inbox receives the verification code, collects the follow-ups in isolation, and then expires — taking the whole marketing chain with it.

The tier decision takes five seconds once it becomes habit, and it prevents most signup spam before it starts. The alias-versus-temp-mail choice within the system is explained in [Temp Email vs Email Alias: Which Protects Privacy Better?](/blog/temp-email-vs-email-alias).

## Setting it up in practice

1. **Reserve your personal email for Tier 1 only.** Stop using it as the default signup address. The default for anything new is Tier 3 unless you have a reason to promote it.
2. **Use one temporary inbox per signup, not one for all signups.** A single shared throwaway inbox recreates the original problem in miniature: mixed messages, no clean retirement, and no way to tell which signup is emailing you.
3. **Name inboxes after the signup.** `coupon-outdoor-store` and `trial-note-app` make triage instant and retirement obvious. The full naming system is in [How to Manage Multiple Temporary Emails for Different Websites](/blog/how-to-manage-multiple-temp-emails).
4. **Pick an expiry that covers the signup's real window.** A coupon needs hours; a trial needs days. A 10-minute inbox that expires before a delayed confirmation arrives has not saved you anything.
5. **Save anything you need immediately.** Order numbers, activation links, and discount codes should be copied to your notes at once. Temporary inboxes expire by design, and anything left only in the inbox is gone with it.

## Keeping the system clean over time

A tier system only works if Tier 3 actually ends. Once a week, glance at your active temporary inboxes: retire the ones whose tasks finished, and migrate anything you decided to keep — a trial you are paying for, a store you now order from regularly — to a Tier 2 alias or your permanent address before expiry. Without that step, the system slowly accumulates inboxes the same way a personal inbox accumulates newsletters.

Also watch for the two leaks that undo the strategy. The first is using your personal address "just this once" for a signup that turns out to be spammy — one exception puts you back on a list. The second is using a temporary inbox for an account that later becomes important, then losing access when it expires. Both are avoided by making the tier decision before signup, not after.

For US shoppers and trial users, the same system pays off in a specific way: US signup flows are heavy on post-purchase and post-trial marketing, from abandoned-cart reminders to win-back campaigns. Routing those through Tier 3 means your personal inbox never becomes the record of every store and trial you ever tried.

## What this strategy does not do

It does not make a spammy site trustworthy, it does not protect an account that needs recovery, and it does not substitute for a permanent address on banking, payment, or identity-critical services. Those still belong in Tier 1. What it does is narrower and more valuable: it keeps the marketing consequences of casual signups quarantined in inboxes that are designed to disappear.

## Bottom line

Stop trying to unsubscribe your way out of signup spam. Sort signups into permanent, alias, and temporary tiers before you sign up, give each temporary signup its own named inbox, and let finished inboxes expire. The spam never reaches your personal inbox, because it was never given the address.
