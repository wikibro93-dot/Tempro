## Why unsubscribing never quite works

Most signup spam is not a single newsletter you can unsubscribe from. It is a chain: one signup feeds a welcome series, which feeds partner offers, which feeds retargeting, and each list has its own unsubscribe process, its own delays, and its own definition of "unsubscribe." You can spend real time opting out and still receive mail from the same company under a different list. The disposable-inbox strategy does not fight that chain — it stops your personal address from entering it in the first place.

The trick is not to use one throwaway address for everything. It is to sort every signup into a tier before you hand over an address, and to let each tier expire, forward, or persist on its own schedule.

## The three-tier system

### Tier 1 — Throwaway: one code, one hour, gone

This is the bottom tier, for signups you will never need again. A coupon unlock on a store you will never revisit, a download gate for a single PDF, a "verify to continue" wall on a site you opened once. The address exists to receive one code or one link, and then it should disappear.

**Services that fit:** TempMail Pro with a 10-minute or 1-hour expiry, Guerrilla Mail, or 10 Minute Mail. These are built for exactly this: no sign-in, an address in seconds, gone quickly. For a coupon that arrives within the hour, TempMail Pro's 1-hour preset is the natural pick; 10 minutes is enough only when the code is instant.

**The rule:** if the only thing you need from the signup is a code or a download link, it belongs here. Never put anything here that you might need to recover.

### Tier 2 — Sampling: a week or a month of watching

The middle tier is where most signup spam actually comes from, and it is the tier most people skip. These are the services you are trying out: a US free trial for a streaming service or a design tool, a new Substack writer, a deal site you might like, a store you might order from again. You need the address alive long enough to judge the service — long enough to receive the trial reminder on day six, the second newsletter, the follow-up offer — but you do not want it alive forever.

**Services that fit:** TempMail Pro is the natural home for this tier, because it is the only one of the common free options with a real duration menu: 7 days for a week-long trial, or a custom duration up to 365 days for a slower evaluation. Give the inbox a custom name for the service — `trial-note-app`, `streaming-test` — so you know what it is a week later. Note the honest caveat: a custom name gets a random 4-character suffix appended, so the address is close to your chosen name rather than exactly it. One inbox per trial, never one shared inbox for all of them: a shared sampling inbox mixes trial reminders together and you cannot retire one trial without killing the others.

**The decision point:** at the end of the sampling window, the service either earns a promotion or it expires. If you are still using it, move it to Tier 3 with a permanent address or an alias. If you stopped opening its mail after day two, let the inbox expire and the whole marketing chain dies with it. That decision — promote or let expire — is the entire system. Without it, Tier 2 slowly becomes a second permanent inbox full of mail you never read.

### Tier 3 — Semi-permanent: an alias you control, for keepers

The top tier is for accounts you have decided to keep, but still do not want tied directly to your personal address. A store you now order from regularly, a tool you pay for, a community you return to weekly. These need recovery, so a temporary inbox is the wrong tool — expired inboxes are assumed unrecoverable, and a password reset sent to a dead address locks you out at the worst moment.

**Services that fit:** an email alias, not a temp inbox. Apple's Hide My Email, SimpleLogin, and Firefox Relay all generate aliases that forward to a mailbox you control, and each alias can be disabled individually if one service starts spamming you. Gmail's `+` addressing (`you+store@gmail.com`) is a lighter version of the same idea, though it is easier for a sender to strip. Your personal address itself belongs only to the small set of services you fully trust: your bank, your employer, services you have used for years.

**The rule:** an account moves to Tier 3 the moment losing access to it would cost you something — money, a subscription, a reputation, a history. That move is a fresh signup or an email change on the service, done while the Tier 2 inbox is still live so you can confirm the change arrived.

## Setting it up in practice with TempMail Pro

1. **Make Tier 1 your default, not your personal address.** Stop using your personal email as the default signup address. The default for anything new is a throwaway inbox unless you have a reason to promote it.
2. **Use one temporary inbox per signup, not one for all signups.** A single shared throwaway inbox recreates the original problem in miniature: mixed messages, no clean retirement, and no way to tell which signup is emailing you.
3. **Name inboxes after the signup.** `coupon-outdoor-store` and `trial-note-app` make triage instant and retirement obvious. The full naming system is in [Temp Mail With Multiple Inboxes Free: Complete Guide](/blog/free-temp-mail-multiple-inboxes-guide).
4. **Pick an expiry that covers the signup's real window.** A coupon needs an hour; a trial needs 7 days or a custom duration. A 10-minute inbox that expires before a delayed confirmation arrives has not saved you anything. TempMail Pro's menu is 10 minutes, 1 hour, 24 hours, 7 days, or custom up to 365 days.
5. **Save anything you need immediately.** Order numbers, activation links, and discount codes should be copied to your notes at once. Temporary inboxes expire by design, and anything left only in the inbox is gone with it.
6. **Run the weekly promotion-or-expire review.** Once a week, glance at your active Tier 2 inboxes: promote the ones you kept to an alias, and let the rest expire. This is the step that keeps the system from rotting into clutter.

## A worked example: one week of US signups

Monday, you try a US meal-kit trial, unlock a 20%-off coupon at an outdoor store, and sample a new Substack writer. Three signups, three inboxes: the coupon gets 1 hour, the trial gets 7 days, the Substack gets a custom 30 days. By Wednesday the coupon inbox is gone. By next Monday the trial inbox has caught the "your trial ends tomorrow" reminder, and you decide the meal kit is not worth it — the inbox expires, and its win-back emails have nowhere to go. The Substack is still being opened, so you resubscribe with an alias and delete the temporary inbox. Your personal inbox received none of the three signups, and none of the three services has your real address.

For US shoppers and trial users, the same system pays off in a specific way: US signup flows are heavy on post-purchase and post-trial marketing, from abandoned-cart reminders to win-back campaigns. Routing those through Tier 1 and Tier 2 means your personal inbox never becomes the record of every store and trial you ever tried.

## What this strategy does not do

It does not make a spammy site trustworthy, it does not protect an account that needs recovery, and it does not substitute for a permanent address on banking, payment, or identity-critical services. Those still belong in Tier 3 with a real address. What it does is narrower and more valuable: it keeps the marketing consequences of casual signups quarantined in inboxes that are designed to disappear.

Two leaks undo the strategy. The first is using your personal address "just this once" for a signup that turns out to be spammy — one exception puts you back on a list. The second is using a temporary inbox for an account that later becomes important, then losing access when it expires. Both are avoided by making the tier decision before signup, not after.

## How this differs from the newsletter approach

Newsletters are one kind of signup, and they have their own workflow — sampling a publication over weeks, then migrating the keepers to a permanent address. This system is wider: it covers every signup, not just newsletters, and its core is the weekly promote-or-expire decision across all three tiers. If newsletters are your main problem, start with [Managing Email Subscriptions: Temporary Email for Newsletters](/blog/temp-email-for-newsletters); if signups in general are the problem, this tier system is the one to run.

## Bottom line

Stop trying to unsubscribe your way out of signup spam. Sort every signup before you sign up — throwaway for one code, sampling for a trial you are judging, semi-permanent alias for a keeper — give each temporary signup its own named inbox with an expiry that matches its real window, and run the weekly promote-or-expire review. The spam never reaches your personal inbox, because it was never given the address.
