## The first question: email code or SMS code?

Before you reach for any tool, check what kind of code the signup actually asks for. They are different problems with different solutions, and mixing them up is the most common source of frustration.

**An email code** arrives in an inbox. You type an email address into the signup form, the service sends a code to it, you read it and enter it. This is the problem a temporary inbox solves. [TempMail Pro](/app) is free, needs no sign-in, and gives you a disposable address for exactly this — no card, no account.

**An SMS code** arrives as a text message to a phone number. You type a phone number into the signup form, the service texts a code to it, you read it on a phone and enter it. No email address — temporary or permanent — can receive an SMS code. A site that requires a phone number will not accept an email address instead, however good the inbox is.

Knowing which kind you need before you start saves the frustration of creating the wrong tool for the job. If a signup asks only for email, a temporary inbox is usually the cleanest option. If it asks for a phone number, you face a separate decision with its own risks, covered below. And if it asks for both, treat them separately: the email part can be temporary while the phone part needs its own judgment call.

## TempMail Pro is email-only — and that is by design

TempMail Pro handles email verification and nothing else. SMS OTP is not a core TempMail Pro feature, and there is no SMS inbox inside the app. That is not a missing feature waiting to be added; it is the boundary of what a temporary email service is. If a service insists on a phone number, TempMail Pro cannot help with that half of the signup, and any article claiming otherwise is misleading you.

The good news is that many signups offer a choice. Before handing over a phone number, check whether the service accepts email verification as an alternative — many do, and email is the safer channel to keep disposable. ## Why public SMS receiver sites are unsafe

Public SMS receiver websites publish shared phone numbers whose incoming messages anyone can read. That is not a privacy tool — it is the opposite of one. Anyone viewing the same number can see your verification code, and a code visible to strangers is barely a verification step at all.

This blog used to include a how-to for those sites. It has been removed, deliberately, because teaching readers to use public receivers on a privacy product's blog was wrong. Here is the honest assessment:

- **Anyone can read your code.** The number is shared, the inbox is public, and there is no login protecting it. Whoever is watching the same number at the same moment sees the same code.
- **Your account can be taken over later.** If the code is visible to strangers, anyone who saw it can use it to reset or access the account — not just at signup, but whenever a code is sent to that number again.
- **The number is reused across strangers.** Codes for different people's accounts arrive in the same public list, which means your signup is entangled with other people's accounts.
- **It fails exactly when it matters.** A public number may work for a throwaway check and then fail for anything real, because services increasingly block numbers known to belong to public receivers.

Never use a public number for banking, payment, account recovery, or any account you care about keeping. In fact, do not use one for anything where losing the account would cost you more than a minute — the saving is small and the exposure is total.

## The honest options when a site insists on SMS

Some platforms simply require a phone number, and no email workaround exists. In that case the honest options are three, and none of them is a public receiver:

1. **Use a personal number you control, and accept the exposure.** This is the right choice for accounts that matter — banking, payments, anything tied to your identity — because the number is yours, the code is private, and you keep the recovery path. The cost is that the service now has your real number.
2. **Use a private virtual number you pay for.** A dedicated virtual number — from a paid service such as Twilio, Hushed, or a similar provider — is yours alone, so no stranger can read your codes. It makes sense when you regularly need phone verification without exposing your personal number, and little sense for a single one-off signup, because of the cost and the new account to manage.
3. **Skip the service.** If the account is not worth your real number or a paid virtual one, that is information. Walk away rather than borrowing a public number for an account you intend to keep — the verification may succeed and the account may still be compromised later by anyone who watched that number.

A secondary SIM or a dedicated number you control can serve as a middle ground for accounts that matter moderately: real, private, and separate from your main number.

## A checklist before you hand over any number

1. Confirm what the number is actually for: one-time signup, ongoing two-factor authentication, or account recovery. Ongoing uses need a number you will keep; one-time uses do not.
2. Never use a shared or public number for sensitive accounts, and never reuse a verification number across banking and casual signups alike.
3. Check whether the service accepts email verification as an alternative — many do, and email is the safer channel to keep disposable.
4. If a code does not arrive, wait before requesting resends in a loop: repeated requests can trigger rate limits that lock the signup entirely, and the original code may still be in transit.
5. Clear up after yourself: remove a temporary number from an account you abandon, and do not leave a working verification path attached to an account you no longer control.
